Caring for a Collection · First-response guide

Before Anything Is Moved

A first-response guide for families, congregations, and community organizations caring for an at-risk collection.

Use this guide when a move, death, closure, storage deadline, or change of caretaker places papers, photographs, recordings, objects, or digital files at risk. The purpose is not to build an archive in a weekend. It is to preserve enough context and control for the next decision to remain reversible.

Medjat ArchivesPublic guidanceVersion 1.0 · August 2026

Start with Safety

Stop and get qualified help

Do not enter or handle materials where there is active fire, exposed electricity, unstable shelving, contaminated standing water, strong chemical odor, extensive mold, broken glass, or another immediate hazard. Protect people first. Contact emergency personnel, a conservator, or an appropriate local authority before attempting collection work.

If the area is safe, begin by slowing the situation down. Ask for a short, explicit pause in discarding, distributing, cleaning, reorganizing, or scanning. Give the pause a clear limit, such as forty-eight hours or one week, so that it creates room for a basic review without appearing to stop the move indefinitely.

The governing rule

Preserve context before improving order. A collection can look neater and become less intelligible at the same time.

The First-Response Sequence

1
Pause disposal and distribution
Stop throwing away, giving away, separating, cleaning, relabeling, or reorganizing materials until the collection has been documented at a basic level.
2
Establish authority
Record who owns the materials, who controls the current location, and who may authorize movement, copying, access, or transfer. Note any organizational, privacy, copyright, cultural, or community authority that may also govern the collection.
3
Document the collection in place
Take wide photographs of the room, shelves, furniture, boxes, labels, and visible groupings before anything changes. Then photograph each container and its label. Do not open restricted material merely to improve the inventory.
4
Record living knowledge
Ask knowledgeable people about creators, custodians, names, nicknames, organizations, events, dates, restrictions, and the way the materials were used. Record who can interpret the collection later.
5
Create a container-level inventory
Give each box, bin, drawer, shelf section, drive, or device a temporary number. Write one short description, an approximate date range, and any condition or access concern. Item-level cataloging can wait.
6
Account for digital materials
List computers, phones, drives, memory cards, email accounts, websites, cloud services, and specialized software. Record who controls access. Keep passwords and recovery information in a separate secure location.
7
Choose a temporary destination
Prefer a dry, secure, accessible, and reasonably stable interior location. Avoid basements, attics, exterior sheds, direct sunlight, active leaks, and areas subject to severe temperature changes.
8
Control the move
Keep existing groupings together. Maintain the temporary numbers. Record which person moved each container, when it left, and where it was placed. Do not redistribute parts of the collection during transport.
9
Document arrival and seek help
Confirm that the same number of containers arrived. Note any damage or missing material. Contact an archive, library, museum, historical society, conservator, records manager, or trusted community organization for advice before making permanent decisions.

Document Before Movement

Photographs should show relationships, not only individual objects. Begin with the doorway and photograph the room in overlapping views. Record each wall, shelf, cabinet, stack, and labeled container. Include enough surrounding space to show where one group ends and another begins.

Keep a simple movement log. A useful entry records the temporary container number, its brief description, its original location, its destination, the date moved, the person responsible, and any problem observed. The inventory needs to show that twenty-three containers left one location and twenty-three arrived at another.

Existing folders, labels, envelopes, albums, boxes, and shelf groupings are evidence. Preserve them even when they appear incomplete or untidy. Keep materials from different people or organizations separate. Do not combine all photographs, correspondence, or books merely because they share a format.

Photography and an IHS survey are different activities. An authorized caretaker may photograph a room and container labels to document an emergency move. During a preliminary IHS intake survey, nothing is photographed or removed unless the collection holder separately authorizes it. The survey does not replace the caretaker's movement record.

Record What Only People Know

Ask while knowledgeable people are available. Paper can sometimes be stabilized. Lost context cannot always be reconstructed.

  • Who created or gathered the materials?
  • Who used them, and for what work?
  • Which names, nicknames, abbreviations, organizations, and events appear repeatedly?
  • Why were the materials arranged this way?
  • Has anything already been removed, distributed, or discarded?
  • Which materials are private, sacred, culturally governed, legally sensitive, or unsafe to circulate?
  • Who else can identify people, places, voices, handwriting, or relationships?
  • Who should be contacted before access, copying, or transfer?

Record both the information and its source. A name supplied by a family member is not the same as a name inferred by a volunteer. If people disagree, preserve the disagreement rather than forcing a single answer.

Handle Less, Not More

Do not begin conservation treatment during an emergency move. Use clean, dry hands for ordinary paper, support fragile materials fully, and handle photographs and negatives by their edges using media-appropriate guidance. Do not force brittle books open, flatten rolled documents, play damaged recordings, or power on wet or visibly failing electronic devices.

Isolate material affected by visible mold or pests from the rest of the collection without attempting casual cleaning. Mold can present health risks and spread through a collection. If material is wet, seek preservation advice quickly because delay can make recovery more difficult.

Do not repairDo not use tape, glue, lamination, household cleaners, heat, water, solvents, or improvised flattening methods.
Do not write on originalsUse separate, reversible labels and temporary container numbers. Do not add names directly to photographs or documents.
Do not remove contextDo not pull photographs from albums, letters from envelopes, papers from books, or labels from boxes merely to make storage uniform.
Do not discard duplicatesFirst check annotations, enclosures, versions, signatures, stamps, and evidence of who received or used each copy.
Do not scan indiscriminatelyA scan without identity, provenance, restrictions, quality control, and maintained storage can create more files without preserving more knowledge.
Do not publish credentialsInventories should identify devices and accounts, but passwords and recovery information belong in a separate secure record available only to authorized successors.

Account for Digital Materials

Digital material should receive the same container-level accounting as boxes and shelves. List devices and accounts without merging everything into one folder. Preserve existing folder names and directory structure because organization can document how the creator worked.

When an authorized copy is made, copy files rather than moving them, retain the original device, document the date and person responsible, and maintain more than one usable copy in different locations. Do not discard a physical original merely because a scan exists. Digitization, appraisal, and disposal are separate decisions.

Record websites, social-media accounts, domains, subscriptions, and cloud services that could lapse. Note which person controls each account and whether a successor or recovery method has been established. Do not place sensitive credentials inside a broadly shared inventory.

If Materials Change Hands

No material should be handed to another person or institution without a dated record of what transferred, who released it, who received it, the authority under which the transfer occurred, the number of containers, and the temporary or permanent terms.

A receipt and container list are the minimum for an emergency transfer. A longer stewardship relationship may require a storage agreement, deed of gift, copyright provisions, access restrictions, return conditions, and a process for changing future instructions. A repository's willingness to advise does not mean that it can accept the collection.

This guide is not a substitute for legal advice, professional conservation treatment, an estate plan, or a written stewardship agreement. It is a method for keeping the collection and its decision-making history intelligible until those forms of help become available.

A One-Page Continuity Plan

Create this plan while the collection is stable and knowledgeable people are available. Review it at least annually and whenever a caretaker, location, account, or risk changes.

  1. What is the collection?
    Describe its creators, subjects, formats, and approximate dates.
  2. Where is it?
    List physical locations, devices, accounts, and backup locations.
  3. Who holds authority?
    Record ownership, custody, organizational standing, and known copyright, privacy, or cultural governance.
  4. Who understands it?
    Name the people who can identify individuals, events, abbreviations, restrictions, and relationships.
  5. Who is responsible now?
    Name a primary caretaker and an alternate.
  6. What are the immediate risks?
    Note storage, water, mold, pests, obsolete media, missing credentials, cost, and pending moves.
  7. What happens during a transition?
    State who must be contacted before material is moved, divided, copied, discarded, or transferred.
  8. What assistance is available?
    List specific repositories, conservators, community organizations, and professionals worth calling.
  9. When will the plan be reviewed?
    Choose a date and record who is responsible for the review.

Sources and Scope

This guide translates established preservation and archival principles into a first-response sequence for community collections. Local conditions, legal authority, cultural protocols, and the nature of the materials may require different action.
